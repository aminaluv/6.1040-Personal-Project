# Problem Framing

### Domain

The main domain in which the problem lies is choosing your next book to read. Whether you are an avid reader or someone looking to get into reading, finding your next good book to read can be surprisingly difficult. If you want to branch out from your usual genres or are looking for something specific, finding a book that resonates with you on a deeper level can feel like finding a needle in a haystack. I used to read regularly, but stopped because I kept struggling to find books that matched what I was actually looking for, and it's been hard to get back into the habit since. I found myself searching through Reddit, Google book reviews, library databases, and even asking Claude to generate lists of books that would match my specifications to find books that truly interested me. I've found a handful of decent books this way, but rarely one that truly resonated with me. This difficulty doesn't only stop you from starting your next book, it can show up mid-book as well. As you near the end of a great book, the dread of having to choose your next book can feel daunting and may affect whether you even pick up another book to read.

### Stakeholders

- New readers: People that are trying to build a reading habit for the first time. They don't know what kinds of books they like, so the sheer number of options can stop them from building a reading habit before they even start. New readers are among the most affected stakeholder because a wrong first pick can convince them that reading "isn't for them".

- Returning readers: People who used to read regularly but fell out of the habit. They no longer have a list of books to read that they are still interested in and feel overwhelmed choosing a book to jump back into reading, so each failed attempt to start up their reading habit can push them further away from reading. 

- Avid readers: Regular readers that know their taste, but struggle to find books that match their specific criteria, or who want to branch out into a new genre and don't know where to start. 

- Friends and social circle: The friends, family, mentors, colleagues, and acquaintances that readers may turn to for book recommendations. These friends are asked for suggestions, and benefit when reading becomes something they can share and talk about.

- Authors (especially lesser-known ones): Lesser-known authors compete for attention against the same handful of popular titles that many people keep promoting and repeating. How readers find their books affect whether their books get discovered. 

- Libraries and bookstores: They provide access to physical copies of the books and they want readers to come back to find titles that they will enjoy. When readers leave empty-handed, the libraries and bookstore lose engagement as well. 

- Online book communities and reviewers: Reddit forums, Goodreads users, and book bloggers who may answer "what should I read next?" questions, so a better book discovery tool could reduce repetitive questions. 

### Bad situations

Being unsure about the next book to read or what book to pick up to get into reading in the first place can result in decision fatigue/paralysis or fear of disappointment, stemming from the paradox of choice. The decision paralysis and fear of choosing the "wrong book" can result in breaking a consistent reading habit and can cause someone to stop reading altogether. In addition, the dread and procrastination resulting from the uncertainty of choosing your next book impacting the overall experience of reading the current one.

1. Situation 1

The first bad situation could be that a reader wants to read a sci-fi mystery novel with characters that are around the same age as the reader that they haven't read before, but don't know any books that match these specifications. They post a question on a subreddit and ask an LLM. They are able to get back 10-20 answers, many of which are the same handful of repeated titles that don't fully match the specific criteria. The reader spends an hour cross-checking reviews, and picks the closest match, but they never end up finishing the book they chose because it wasn't what they were looking for. This results in lost time, an unfinished book, and a situation that makes the reader less likely to want to read the specific genre of books they were looking for. 

2. Situation 2

The second bad situation could be that a reader has not read consistently for over 4 years now and they want to start reading again. They don't have a list of books they want to read anymore and don't know what to look for when browsing the library shelves. The reader is completely overwhelmed with the sheer number of options and the lack of a clear way to compare them meaningfully. The reader leaves the library unable to pick a book, and they tell themself that they would come back to it after doing more research, but don't end up opening a book for a few more months. 

3. Situation 3

The third bad situation could be that a reader is almost done with a book that they absolutely loved, but instead of enjoying the ending, the reader starts to dread looking for the next book they are going to read. They open their list of books that other people had recommended them, but close it after scrolling through it for a few minutes, unable to choose a book. They then finish their current book that day, without their next book chosen. Instead of starting a book, a couple weeks pass with no reading. This results in a habit that gets interrupted just as it was working the best. 

### Corroboration

The difficulty of choosing a next book and its effect on readers can be seen in blog posts, questions posted on community forums, and research. On the [ReadingHabit blog post](https://readinghabit.site/blog/how-to-choose-your-next-book), the author mentions the paradox of choice, where more options don't produce better decisions, they often produce no decision at all. The author talks about relying on short, low-commitment books to avoid the disappointment of choosing "wrong". We can see similar sentiments in forums such as [Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/52book/comments/14ff59b/does_anyone_else_have_an_extremely_hard_time/), [Facebook groups](https://www.facebook.com/groups/983616945825132/posts/1690885835098236/), and [The Worm Hole](https://wormhole.carnelianvalley.com/why-do-we-spend-so-long-deciding-what-to-read-instead-of-just-picking-something-up/). There is a report by [Australia Reads](https://australiareads.org.au/news/book-browsing-barrier/) that states that many infrequent readers get stuck at the process of finding a book to read, not being they don't like reading, but because they don't enjoy or prioritize the process of browsing for a book. However, it is good to note that this report notices that many avid readers tend to enjoy the process of browsing for books rather than finding it burdensome like many infrequent readers. This suggests that the bad situation may be less about book choice being inherently hard, but rather difficult for a specific segment of readers who haven't developed browsing as a skill or habit.

### Workarounds and comparables

A few workarounds that people may use to address this issue include utilizing forums such as Reddit to get recommendations for good books to read that meet the person's specifications, getting recommendations and seeing your friends' bookshelves on Goodreads, getting book recommendations from friends, mentors, parents, professors, or colleagues, browsing book stores or libraries in person or online, book subscription services, or even asking LLMs to generate book recommendations based on specifications that the reader is looking for.

There are a handful of solutions that exist for this problem, including [Booky Call](https://www.buzzfeed.com/farrahpenn/booky-call-app-for-book-lovers-like-tinder-review) which is an app that mimics a dating app, but instead of pairing you with a person, the app pairs you with a book. When using Booky Call, you can also swipe left or right based on your interest and the app sends you notifications to suggest your next read. With Booky Call, I think the idea of mimicing a dating app has incredible merit and the idea of matching with a book may create more interest in the suggested book, but the descriptions of the books in the app are fairly long-winded and personified so that the book seems like a person that you are matching with. Additionally, Booky Call doesn't seem to take into account the books that the reader has read before and since the app matches you with a book, the scope of available books that it suggests is slightly limited.

Another solution that exists for this problem is [Bookist](https://bookist.app/#top) which is a similar app that has the swiping feature, where you can swipe right if you like the book and swipe left otherwise. This app keeps track of all the books that you liked, skipped, read, currently reading, want to read, and didn't finish. The log of books can be useful users to know which books they liked and want to read; however, the app only provides a short summary of the book and the book cover as the information you use to determine whether to swipe right or left. I found this to contradict the saying of "don't judge a book by its cover" since the user is required to make a decision based on very limited information about the book.

**Topic:** Book Recommendation App

### Solution sketch

A possible solution would be to create a book app that creates a "feed" of books that you might like based on your previously read or liked books while also keeping track of the books that you find interesting and allows you to see the book that your friends like. In your book "feed" you can swipe right if you like a book and swipe left if you are not interested in the book. However, instead of showing just a short summary of each book and the cover, the user would be able to see details about the book characters (like age range) and setting, radar charts measuring the qualities of the book based on the user's inputted criteria for what they consider a good book, and basic information about the book such as genre, author, summary of the book, published date, etc. This information would help the user make informed decisions about whether to swipe right or left on the book. 

In order to get an accurate understanding of the user, the user would need to make a profile that includes information about what they consider to be a good book, genre preferences and past favorites. After the user makes a profile, they would then be able to invite their friends and follow other users or friends to see the books that others find interesting.

**How each feature addresses the bad situations:**

- **First Impressions (detailed book cards with personal radar charts) -> Situation 1** The reader looking for a sci-fi mystery with characters their own age can see the genre, setting, and character ages on every card, and the radar chart shows how well the book fits their criteria. Instead of spending hours cross-checking reviews and picking the closest match that they never finish, they can tell in seconds whether a book truly fits. This also gives the returning reader in Situation 2 a clear way to compare books that the library shelves lack.

- **Meet Cute Matchmaker (swipe feed) -> Situation 2** The returning reader overwhelmed by library shelves sees one book at a time, matched to their profile and filtered to books available at their library, rather than thousands of options at once. Each swipe is a yes or no, and the feed learns from every choice, so the reader can build a list of books they actually want without having to spend hours researching first.

- **Up Next and Shared Shelves -> Situation 3** Every right swipe and every book saved from a friend's shelf goes to the reader's Up Next shelf, so when they finish a book they love, their next read is already waiting on their shelf. The gap between books, when reading habits usually break, goes away.
